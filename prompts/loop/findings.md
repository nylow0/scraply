# Take stock and propose what comes next

The pass is over: the idea died, survived, or ran out of decisive tests. The inputs hold the idea, every claim with its status and verdict notes, and the saved quotes.

- **summary:** in three to five sentences, what was tested, what survived, what died, and what is still unknown. Untested is not survived. If tests could not run, name exactly what blocked them, such as a data source that refused the query, or a claim marked untestable with what the owner has.
- **findings:** observations the evidence showed that matter beyond the verdicts, especially a surviving signal inside a dead claim, a group of people with a sharper version of the problem, or an existing solution's specific failure. Each cites the quote IDs it rests on.
- **variants:** one to three next versions of the seed, each an idea stated as the thing itself (the product, or the prediction and what it beats), not a study or a process step. Each has a title, a pitch of two to four sentences naming who it is for or what it predicts, and **why** the evidence points there.
  - **Where to look.** Read every listed source (the newest good run of each experiment) in full: every cut with a positive held-out estimate is either taken up by a variant or ruled out in the summary. Propose the variant the best expert in the field would bet on, and look at what this pass found through the five angles in the method: the surviving signal turned into the project, or carried where it should be strongest or where nobody has tested it (a shorter horizon, a smaller area, a sharper audience, a different target or measure); a method borrowed from another field; a new tool or dataset on the same question; an assumption the evidence put in doubt; or a pairing with an unrelated field the evidence hinted at. Two more sources: a version whose claims can be tested with what the owner has when some could not, and the same idea one rung up the ladder, stated as the bigger claim it would then make (one that holds on new held-out data, for a broader population, or against what already exists). When a variant comes from an angle, **why** names what the angle brings, such as the field borrowed from, the untested group, the new tool, or the assumption, rather than only the angle's name.
  - **What it keeps.** The owner's seed stays recognizable, and so does its core output: dropping what the owner wanted, such as the warning or the forecast, is a retreat, not a variant. Each variant names the closest existing product or method from **priorArt** and the measurable way it would beat it; a variant that rebuilds something the prior art already describes is not new. A variant never contradicts the quotes it cites.
  - **What it rests on.** A variant cites, by quote ID, an experiment cut that replicated on both the development and the held-out data or an observation quoted from a page that is not experiment output, or it states the expert judgment it rests on, such as where this kind of signal is known to be strong. **why** says which part comes from this pass's evidence and which is expert judgment still to be tested. Bold is fine: the next pass tests it.
  - **Not a variant:** re-running a test that failed (leave that to the loop), or repeating the same test on the same rung: auditing, reproducing, reframing, or cleaning it up.
  - If nothing survived and no finding points anywhere, return no variants and say so in the summary.
